## 1. Admiralty/NATO grading

Grade the **source** and the **information** independently, and always write them as a pair (`B2`,
`D4`). The whole point is that the axes are separable: a reliable outlet can repeat a rumor, and a
notorious one can publish a document that checks out.

**Source reliability**

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Completely reliable — long history, no known failures, authoritative for this domain |
| B | Usually reliable — mostly accurate record, occasional errors |
| C | Fairly reliable — mixed record |
| D | Not usually reliable — frequent errors, but not worthless |
| E | Unreliable — demonstrated pattern of inaccuracy or fabrication |
| F | Cannot be judged — no basis for assessment (a new account, an anonymous tip) |

**Information credibility**

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Confirmed by independent sources, logically consistent with everything known |
| 2 | Probably true — consistent with other information, not independently confirmed |
| 3 | Possibly true — reasonably consistent, some corroboration |
| 4 | Doubtful — not confirmed, and in tension with other information |
| 5 | Improbable — contradicted by other information |
| 6 | Cannot be judged |

Usage rules that matter more than the tables:

- **F6 is a legitimate and common grade.** Refusing to write it — inflating an unassessable source
  to "C3" so the report looks fuller — is the most frequent abuse of this system.
- Independence is the hard part of grade 1. Six outlets running one wire story is one source.
  Two databases mirroring one registry is one source.
- A source's grade is domain-specific. An outlet that is A for its own financial disclosures may be
  D for events in a country it has no presence in.
- When a source is graded on its history, say what that history is. An ungrounded grade is an opinion
  dressed as a measurement.

## 2. The probability lexicon

Use these terms, and only these, for likelihood. Consistent vocabulary is what lets a reader compare
two judgments written months apart by different analysts.

| Term | Range |
|---|---|
| almost no chance / remote | 1–5% |
| very unlikely / highly improbable | 5–20% |
| unlikely / improbable | 20–45% |
| roughly even chance | 45–55% |
| likely / probable | 55–80% |
| very likely / highly probable | 80–95% |
| almost certain(ly) | 95–99% |

Rules:
- Never mix a term with a numeric percentage in the same sentence — readers anchor to the number and
  treat it as measured precision that doesn't exist.
- Never use "possible," "could," "may," or "cannot be ruled out" as a likelihood. They are true of
  nearly everything and communicate zero, while *sounding* like a finding. If something is genuinely
  unassessable, say so and explain why rather than reaching for a soft word.
- Do not use these terms for things that already happened and are documented. Facts are facts;
  probability language belongs to judgments.

## 3. Confidence — a separate axis

Likelihood is how probable the judgment is. Confidence is how much weight the basis can bear.
They are independent, and conflating them is the defect that most reliably misleads a decision-maker.

| Confidence | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **High** | Multiple independent, well-graded sources; corroborated across artifact classes; few gaps bearing on the judgment; the reasoning survives a devil's-advocate pass |
| **Moderate** | Credible sourcing with some corroboration; meaningful gaps exist; plausible alternatives remain but are weaker |
| **Low** | Sparse, single-source, poorly graded, or heavily gap-dependent; the judgment shifts materially if one item fails |

Write both, always, in this form:

> It is **likely** that the two properties are operated by the same party (**low confidence**) —
> the judgment rests on a single shared analytics identifier and no independent corroboration.

That sentence is honest and useful. "Probably the same operator" is neither.

**Low confidence + high likelihood is not a failure state.** It is often the correct product, and
reporting it plainly is what preserves the analyst's credibility when the case later collapses.

## 4. Analytic standards

Every product should satisfy these. They are worth checking off explicitly before delivery:

1. **Objective** — no advocacy; the judgment does not lean toward what the customer wants.
2. **Independent of political consideration** — the answer is not adjusted for who it embarrasses.
3. **Timely** — a perfect assessment after the decision is a historical document.
4. **Based on all available sources** — including the ones that undercut the conclusion.
5. **Distinguishes evidence from judgment** — the four-label rule enforces this at sentence level.
6. **Expresses uncertainty properly** — lexicon plus separate confidence.
7. **Distinguishes changes from prior judgments** — when an assessment shifts, say what changed and
   why. A reader tracking a case over time needs the delta, not a fresh document that quietly
   contradicts last month's.
8. **Attributes sources accurately** — with grades, and with their limitations stated.
9. **Logically argued** — the inference chain is visible and each link inspectable.
10. **Incorporates alternatives** — a product with one explanation has not been analyzed, only written.

## 5. Writing rules and banned language

- **Never a verb of agency you cannot support.** "Coordinated," "directed," "funded," "controlled,"
  "orchestrated," and "operated by" are rung-3 claims (see below). At rung 1, the verbs available
  are "shares," "is hosted alongside," "published identical content to."
- **Ban vagueness that implies precision:** "sources indicate," "it is widely believed," "reports
  suggest." Name the source and grade it, or drop the claim.
- **Ban "origin," "patient zero," "first."** Write "earliest observed in collected data" — the
  difference is not pedantry, it is the difference between a statement about the world and a
  statement about your collection.
- **Ban conclusions that cannot be falsified.** If no evidence would change it, it is a belief.
- Prefer short declarative sentences with one claim each. Compound sentences smuggle unsupported
  clauses past the reader on the back of supported ones.

## 6. The linkage/attribution ladder

Record explicitly which rung each conclusion sits on, and never let language drift upward between
the evidence section and the summary — that drift is the single most common way a defensible report
becomes an indefensible headline.

| Rung | Claim | Requires |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Association** | These entities share an artifact | The artifact, its discriminating power, and the mundane explanations for it |
| **2 — Common operation** | The same hands run them | Multiple independent artifact classes, temporal consistency, elimination of shared-infrastructure and coincidence explanations |
| **3 — Attribution** | A named actor is responsible | Everything above, plus evidence tying the operation to that actor specifically, plus an explicit deception assessment explaining why this is not a false flag |

Most sound work lives at rung 1 or 2. Stopping there and saying so is a stronger product than
reaching rung 3 on inference — and it is the version that survives contact with a hostile reader.
